## Q1: The Right to Leave: Apostasy, Dissent, and the Cost of Doubt

### Judaism
**✓ Evidence of good**:
- No legal death penalty for apostasy in modern Israel or Jewish communities; full freedom of religion protected by law.
- Reform and Conservative branches fully accept leaving without sanction.
- No documented executions for apostasy in over 2,000 years.

**✗ Evidence of harm**:
- Biblical prescriptions for death penalty (Deuteronomy 13); historical rabbinic discussions, though rarely if ever enforced.
- Ultra-Orthodox communities practice social shunning and family severance for apostates.
- Documented cases of ostracism and psychological pressure in strict sects.

**Current status**: Apostasy carries no legal penalties; social costs limited to ultra-Orthodox minorities.

### Christianity
**✓ Evidence of good**:
- Vast majority of denominations allow free exit without formal punishment.
- No Christian-majority countries retain death penalty for apostasy.
- Many denominations actively support religious freedom.

**✗ Evidence of harm**:
- Historical executions for heresy during Inquisition and Reformation eras.
- Some fundamentalist sects practice shunning (e.g., Jehovah's Witnesses, Amish).
- Psychological pressure and family estrangement in conservative communities.

**Current status**: Generally free to leave with no legal consequences; social ostracism varies by sect.

### Islam Sunni
**✓ Evidence of good**:
- Many Sunni-majority countries (e.g., Turkey, Indonesia) do not enforce death penalty.
- Reform scholars argue Quran prescribes no worldly punishment for apostasy.
- Some communities tolerate private disbelief.

**✗ Evidence of harm**:
- Traditional fiqh in all four Sunni schools prescribes death penalty.
- Blasphemy laws used as proxy (Pakistan: frequent imprisonments, mob violence per HRW).
- Social ostracism and family rejection widespread.

**Current status**: Legal enforcement varies; death penalty on books in several countries but rarely executed; social and vigilante threats persist.

### Islam Shia (Twelver incl. Velayat-e Faqih)
**✓ Evidence of good**:
- Some Shia scholars historically argued against death penalty.
- Reform voices advocate freedom of belief.

**✗ Evidence of harm**:
- Iranian law prescribes death for apostasy from Islam; documented executions and imprisonments (Amnesty, HRW).
- Blasphemy/apostasy charges used against dissidents.
- Severe family and social exclusion.

**Current status**: Actively enforced in Iran with executions and long sentences; high risk for public apostasy.

### Salafism / Wahhabism
**✓ Evidence of good**:
- Rare reformist voices question worldly punishment.

**✗ Evidence of harm**:
- Strict adherence to death penalty in traditional texts.
- Enforced in Saudi Arabia (death on books, imprisonments documented by HRW).
- Promotes vigilante action and severe social ostracism.

**Current status**: Death penalty retained and occasionally applied; strongest enforcement among contemporary movements.

## Q2: The Body Count: Violence, War, and the Sanctity of Human Life

### Judaism
**✓ Evidence of good**:
- Modern Jewish teachings emphasize peace and protection of life (pikuach nefesh).
- Israel’s military rules of engagement include civilian protections.

**✗ Evidence of harm**:
- Biblical commands for conquest and destruction of Canaanite populations.
- Historical rare instances of zealot violence (e.g., Sicarii).
- Limited political power historically reduced large-scale violence.

**Current status**: No mainstream theological justifications for offensive holy war; violence limited to national defense context.

### Christianity
**✓ Evidence of good**:
- Pacifist traditions (Quakers, Mennonites); just war theory limits violence.
- Modern churches led anti-war and reconciliation efforts.

**✗ Evidence of harm**:
- Crusades (estimated 1–3 million deaths across campaigns).
- Inquisition executions (thousands); colonial forced conversions.
- Pogroms against Jews facilitated in medieval Europe.

**Current status**: Offensive holy war doctrines retired in mainstream denominations; violence not theologically mandated.

### Islam Sunni
**✓ Evidence of good**:
- Defensive jihad emphasized by many scholars.
- Historical protection of non-combatants in some rulings.

**✗ Evidence of harm**:
- Early conquests and expansion wars (estimates tens of millions over centuries, disputed).
- Modern terrorism by Sunni extremist groups (e.g., ISIS).
- Sectarian violence against Shia.

**Current status**: Offensive jihad doctrines remain available in traditional texts; used by extremists but rejected by mainstream.

### Islam Shia (Twelver incl. Velayat-e Faqih)
**✓ Evidence of good**:
- Emphasis on defensive struggle in mainstream teaching.

**✗ Evidence of harm**:
- Iran-backed militias and proxy wars.
- Historical Safavid forced conversions.
- State-sponsored suppression of dissent.

**Current status**: Velayat-e Faqih justifies state violence; jihad doctrines active in regime ideology.

### Salafism / Wahhabism
**✓ Evidence of good**:
- Rare calls for defensive action only.

**✗ Evidence of harm**:
- Wahhabi campaigns in 18th–19th century Arabia (massacres documented).
- Ideological basis for modern jihadist groups (al-Qaeda, ISIS).
- Promotion of takfir leading to sectarian killings. 

**Current status**: Offensive jihad central to ideology; actively invoked by adherents.

## Q3: The Status of Women: Reverence in Words vs. Reality in Law

### Judaism
**✓ Evidence of good**:
- Reform/Conservative branches grant full equality in leadership and divorce.
- Israel ranks mid-high on global gender gap indices (WEF).

**✗ Evidence of harm**:
- Orthodox law: women’s testimony worth less in some cases; agunot (chained wives) issue.
- Inheritance favors males in traditional halakha.
- Gender segregation in ultra-Orthodox communities.

**Current status**: Equality in progressive branches and secular Israel; persistent inequality in Orthodox sectors.

### Christianity
**✓ Evidence of good**:
- Many denominations ordain women; egalitarian theology widespread.
- Christian-majority countries often top gender equality rankings.

**✗ Evidence of harm**:
- Historical subordination texts used to deny rights.
- Some conservative sects restrict women’s leadership and divorce.
- Domestic violence higher in some fundamentalist communities studier.

**Current status**: Broad reform achieved; inequality persists in conservative denominations.

### Islam Sunni
**✓ Evidence of good**:
- Some countries advancing reforms (Tunisia equal inheritance).
- Scholarly arguments for contextual reinterpretation.

**✗ Evidence of harm**:
- Traditional law: inheritance half of male; testimony half in financial matters.
- Polygamy permitted; divorce easier for men.
- Low gender gap rankings in many Sunni countries (WEF).

**Current status**: Structural inequalities remain in most legal systems; reforms slow and contested.

### Islam Shia (Twelver incl. Velayat-e Faqih)
**✓ Evidence of good**:
- Limited reforms in personal status in some communities.

**✗ Evidence of harm**:
- Iran: compulsory hijab; unequal inheritance/testimony.
- Temporary marriage (mut’ah) criticized as exploitative.
- Low gender equality metrics.

**Current status**: State-enforced gender hierarchy under guardianship laws.

### Salafism / Wahhabism
**✓ Evidence of good**:
- Minimal; emphasis on “protection” claimed.

**✗ Evidence of harm**:
- Strict gender segregation; male guardianship.
- Saudi (historically Wahhabi-influenced): driving ban until 2018.
- Lowest gender equality outcomes.

**Current status**: Most restrictive interpretation enforced where influential.
