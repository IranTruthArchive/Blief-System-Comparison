You’re asking for a serious comparative audit, so I’ll keep it evidence-first and consistent, but also concise enough to stay usable.

## Scope and method

* Systems covered: **Judaism, Christianity, Sunni Islam, Shia Islam (Twelver incl. Velayat-e Faqih), Salafism/Wahhabism**.
* Standard applied: texts + jurisprudence + historical governance + present-day enforcement.
* I distinguish:

  1. **doctrinal core** (scripture/legal tradition),
  2. **institutional interpretation** (churches, rabbinates, fiqh schools, clerical states),
  3. **state practice** (what governments and courts actually do).
* Where reliable quantitative cross-system data is weak, I state that explicitly instead of pretending precision.

## Q1: Right to Leave — Apostasy, Dissent, Blasphemy

### Judaism

**✓ Evidence of good**

* Mainstream modern Jewish denominations (Reform, Conservative, much of Modern Orthodox) generally do **not** operate criminal apostasy structures in modern states.
* Contemporary Jewish communities in liberal democracies mostly process exit as social/religious identity change, not state prosecution.

**✗ Evidence of harm**

* Classical halakhic texts include severe penalties for idolatry/blasphemy in a theocratic legal frame (historical-theological legacy).
* In some tightly closed ultra-Orthodox communities, documented social sanctions for leavers (shunning, family rupture) can be severe.

**Current status**: Usually non-criminal in modern state law, but social penalties can remain high in insular communities.

### Christianity

**✓ Evidence of good**

* Post–Vatican II Catholic doctrine explicitly affirms religious freedom and immunity from coercion in matters of belief. ([Vatican][1])
* Most contemporary Christian-majority constitutional orders no longer criminalize apostasy.

**✗ Evidence of harm**

* Historical record includes heresy prosecutions/inquisitorial coercion under church-state fusion.
* Formal church apologies acknowledge grave historical abuses (important for institutional reckoning, though incomplete). ([CBS News][2])

**Current status**: In most countries, apostasy is legally protected; coercive patterns persist mainly in fringe sectarian settings.

### Islam Sunni

**✓ Evidence of good**

* Some Sunni scholars/states now argue for narrowed/non-capital readings of apostasy (especially distinguishing political treason from private belief).
* Reformist Muslim intellectual currents support freedom of conscience.

**✗ Evidence of harm**

* Classical Sunni fiqh schools widely preserve apostasy/blasphemy punishability in legal tradition.
* Contemporary enforcement persists in multiple jurisdictions with blasphemy/apostasy laws; restrictions correlate with high religious-freedom constraints. ([Pew Research Center][3])
* Documented prosecutions and rights abuses tied to blasphemy/apostasy frameworks remain active in several states. ([uscirf.gov][4])

**Current status**: Highly mixed; liberal reinterpretations exist, but state enforcement remains real in a nontrivial set of countries.

### Islam Shia (Twelver incl. Velayat-e Faqih)

**✓ Evidence of good**

* Internal Shia reformist scholarship exists (minority but real), arguing for more rights-compatible interpretations.
* Some Shia-majority contexts outside theocratic enforcement are less punitive.

**✗ Evidence of harm**

* In the Iranian model, dissent/apostasy-adjacent expression can be prosecuted via broad religious-security charges; repression of religious and political dissent is documented.
* Gender-policing and ideological enforcement are tied to clerical-legal authority. ([Human Rights Watch][5])

**Current status**: In the Velayat-e Faqih system, coercive enforcement is ongoing and structurally embedded.

### Salafism / Wahhabism

**✓ Evidence of good**

* Some contemporary Salafi currents are politically quietist and reject vigilante violence.
* Charitable/service networks exist in some communities.

**✗ Evidence of harm**

* Doctrinal exclusivism plus punitive apostasy/blasphemy readings remain influential in parts of this spectrum.
* Rights groups document legal frameworks that still allow severe penalties (including death-eligible apostasy/blasphemy provisions in Saudi draft penal context). ([Amnesty International][6])

**Current status**: Reform rhetoric has increased in places, but coercive legal architecture remains significant.

## Q2: Violence, War, Civilian Protection

### Judaism

**✓ Evidence of good**

* Strong ethical streams (pikuach nefesh, just-war constraints in later rabbinic thought) can function as restraint.
* Modern Jewish thought includes robust pacifist/human-rights voices.

**✗ Evidence of harm**

* Biblical conquest/herem texts have been historically available for maximalist readings.
* In modern ethno-religious conflict contexts, religious nationalism can intensify dehumanization and legitimation narratives.

**Current status**: Normatively plural; outcomes depend heavily on state nationalism, legal constraints, and political leadership, not scripture alone.

### Christianity

**✓ Evidence of good**

* Strong peace-building traditions (e.g., churches in reconciliation, anti-war advocacy, humanitarian networks).
* Modern mainstream doctrine in many churches sharply limits legitimate force and elevates human rights.

**✗ Evidence of harm**

* Crusades, confessional wars, inquisitions, forced conversions are major historical harms.
* Long lag between moral harm recognition and institutional reform.

**Current status**: Large-scale church-endorsed holy war doctrine is marginal in mainstream Christianity today.

### Islam Sunni

**✓ Evidence of good**

* Classical legal traditions include rules intended to limit wartime conduct.
* Major mainstream Sunni institutions denounce modern jihadist terrorism.

**✗ Evidence of harm**

* Textual and juristic material can be (and has been) used for expansionist or punitive violence under certain readings.
* Contemporary extremist mobilization often draws selectively from Sunni legal-theological vocabularies.

**Current status**: Mainstream Sunni institutions mostly reject terrorism, but violent doctrinal exploitation remains a live risk.

### Islam Shia (Twelver incl. Velayat-e Faqih)

**✓ Evidence of good**

* Doctrines of patience/occultation historically constrained revolutionary violence in many periods.
* Some Shia scholars advocate constitutionalism and pluralism.

**✗ Evidence of harm**

* Under clerical-state fusion, coercive security doctrine can sacralize repression and proxy militarization.
* Suppression of protest and dissent has been extensively documented in Iran. ([Human Rights Watch][7])

**Current status**: In the Iranian model, religion-state fusion has repeatedly aligned with coercive political violence.

### Salafism / Wahhabism

**✓ Evidence of good**

* Quietist strands can reduce insurgent violence.
* Some official programs now promote counter-extremism narratives.

**✗ Evidence of harm**

* Takfiri/exclusionary subcurrents have provided doctrinal scaffolding for severe anti-outgroup violence in modern militant movements.
* Anti-blasphemy/apostasy frameworks reinforce coercive social order. ([Amnesty International][6])

**Current status**: Spectrum is internally split; nonviolent quietism coexists with ideas historically weaponized by extremists.

## Q3: Women — Law vs lived outcomes

### Judaism

**✓ Evidence of good**

* Women’s education and leadership expanded significantly in non-Haredi denominations.
* Legal reforms in some rabbinic courts and state systems attempt to mitigate gender harms.

**✗ Evidence of harm**

* In Orthodox halakhic divorce, get-refusal (agunah) remains a structurally gendered harm in many cases.
* In conservative religious communities, role restrictions and modesty control can limit autonomy. ([jewishideas.org][8])

**Current status**: Highly denomination-dependent; substantial progress in liberal branches, persistent inequality in stricter legal communities.

### Christianity

**✓ Evidence of good**

* Major contributions by Christian women’s movements to education/health/social reform.
* Many Protestant traditions ordain women; Catholic lay participation expanded.

**✗ Evidence of harm**

* Long exclusion from clergy and authority in many churches.
* Historical use of doctrine to enforce patriarchal family and sexuality norms.

**Current status**: Broad improvement in many churches; major institutions (e.g., Catholic/Orthodox) still restrict ordination.

### Islam Sunni

**✓ Evidence of good**

* Historically, women had defined property/inheritance rights in Islamic law earlier than some contemporaneous societies.
* Contemporary Muslim feminist jurisprudence is active in multiple countries.

**✗ Evidence of harm**

* Classical inheritance/testimony asymmetries and male-guardianship patterns persist in many legal systems/practices.
* Documented constraints on dress autonomy, movement, family law equality in several Sunni-majority jurisdictions.

**Current status**: Strong reform pressure exists, but legal inequality remains widespread in family-status law domains.

### Islam Shia (Twelver incl. Velayat-e Faqih)

**✓ Evidence of good**

* High female educational attainment in Iran in many fields.
* Active women-led reform movements.

**✗ Evidence of harm**

* Compulsory hijab enforcement, legal asymmetries, and punishment architecture are documented.
* Crackdowns on women’s rights defenders continue. ([Human Rights Watch][5])

**Current status**: Significant female social capability coexists with coercive legal-political control over bodily autonomy and dissent.

### Salafism / Wahhabism

**✓ Evidence of good**

* Recent state-level changes in some Gulf contexts improved women’s mobility/work participation.
* Some educational access expanded.

**✗ Evidence of harm**

* Persistent male-guardianship legacies, strict gender segregation norms, and punitive moral policing historically rooted in this tradition.
* Rights groups continue to report structural legal vulnerabilities. ([Amnesty International][6])

**Current status**: Partial top-down reforms, but underlying jurisprudential inequality remains incompletely resolved.

### Judaism

**Status**: Identity-forming narrative tradition more than modern empirically reconstructable biography.

**✗** Canon formation and doctrinal consolidation were historically political-ecclesial processes.

### Islam Sunni

**✓** Early biographical and hadith sciences developed rigorous internal authenticity methods.
**✗** Modern historical criticism disputes parts of transmission chronology and later canonization dynamics.
**Status**: Strong internal method, contested by external critical historiography.

### Islam Shia (Twelver incl. Velayat-e Faqih)

**✓** Rich imam-centered intellectual tradition with deep jurisprudential development.
**✗** Authority claims can centralize interpretive power and limit falsifiability of core premises.
**Status**: Theological coherence internally, limited external historical falsifiability.

### Salafism / Wahhabism

**✓** Claims restoration of earliest Islam.
**✗** Often minimizes historical plurality and later interpretive diversity.
**Status**: High certainty via reductionist reconstruction of early normativity.

**Status**: Best outcomes occur under liberal constitutional constraints.

**✓** Many historically Christian societies today score high on human development under secular-democratic governance.

## Q11: Capacity for reform — internal correction or external pressure?

### Judaism

**✓ Evidence of reform**

* Strong decentralized interpretive tradition allows localized adaptation.
* Major modern reforms (egalitarian practice in non-Orthodox branches) emerged internally.

**✗ Limits**

* Orthodoxy/non-Orthodoxy split shows reform legitimacy is contested.
* In some sectors, halakhic change pace is slow on gender and authority issues.

**Current status**: Reform is possible but fragmented by denominational legitimacy boundaries.

### Christianity

**✓ Evidence of reform**

* Major doctrinal-development episodes (including religious freedom emphasis) show internal correction capacity. ([Vatican][1])
* Public apologies for historical wrongs signal some institutional reckoning. ([CBS News][2])

**✗ Limits**

* Many reversals came after long external pressure and social change.
* Centralized structures can delay correction for generations.

**Current status**: High reform capacity in some denominations, slower in centralized/tradition-heavy institutions.

### Islam Sunni

**✓ Evidence of reform**

* No single magisterium allows plural reform initiatives.
* Contemporary maqasid/public-interest approaches support rights-compatible reinterpretation.

**✗ Limits**

* Lack of central authority also means no definitive closure of punitive premodern rulings.
* State-backed conservative establishments can freeze reform.

**Current status**: Reform is possible but uneven; reversibility risk remains.

### Islam Shia (Twelver incl. Velayat-e Faqih)

**✓ Evidence of reform**

* Ijtihad tradition theoretically permits reinterpretation.
* Reformist clerical voices exist.

**✗ Limits**

* In Velayat-e Faqih, political-theological centralization constrains dissenting reinterpretation.
* Reformers can face repression or exclusion.

**Current status**: Theological tools for reform exist, but institutional power structure limits their effect.

### Salafism / Wahhabism

**✓ Evidence of reform**

* Some policy-level modernization has occurred.
* Selected scholars/states now distance from extremist appropriations.

**✗ Limits**

* Core scriptural-literalist posture resists broad hermeneutic revision.
* Reform often appears administrative/pragmatic rather than deep doctrinal recalibration.

**Current status**: Partial reform without full theological restructuring.

## Bottom-line comparative read (one-paragraph synthesis)

Across these five systems, the strongest predictor of harm is **not mere identity label** but **fusion of sacred authority with coercive state power**, plus weak rights protections for dissenters, women, and minorities. Systems perform best where they are embedded in **constitutional pluralism, independent courts, free press, and freedom of exit**; they perform worst where doctrine is criminal law and criticism is treated as treason to God. Current evidence particularly flags ongoing coercive enforcement risks in jurisdictions using hardline blasphemy/apostasy and gender-control frameworks. ([Pew Research Center][3])
